Why 25 billable hours is optimistic for many freelancers

Admin, invoicing, sales calls, proposals, unpaid revisions and learning are all work, but none of it can be invoiced. A freelancer billing 25 hours a week is already running a 40-hour week, and the months with no work still have to be paid for by the months with work. That is what the buffer row is doing.

The "effective hourly rate" row is the honest comparison with a salaried job: it spreads the same money across 40 hours instead of the billable ones. If that number is below what an employer would pay for the same work, freelancing is currently costing you money.

What this calculator does not include

  • Tax rules specific to your country — the rate you enter is an estimate you should check with an accountant.
  • Pension contributions, health insurance or parental leave beyond what you put in expenses.
  • Scope creep inside a fixed-price project. That is what the project quote part of the workbook is for.
  • Payment delays and bad debt, which the buffer partly covers but cannot remove.

Quoting a fixed-price project?

An hourly rate does not tell you what to charge for "a website" or "a 10-page report". The quote calculator turns estimated hours, revision rounds, risk and your target margin into a single fixed price — and shows the floor below which the project is not worth taking.

Frequently asked

Should I charge more than my employee equivalent?

Usually yes, because you are also covering the employer's side: tools, insurance, unpaid admin, unpaid holiday and gaps between contracts. The comparison row shows that gap directly.

Is a day rate just eight hours?

Only if you actually bill eight hours. Many freelancers price a day at six billable hours and leave room for calls and admin, which is why the day rate here is shown separately from the hourly rate.

Why add a buffer on top of expenses?

Because revenue is uneven. The buffer is what turns an average year into a survivable one when two clients pay late in the same month.
